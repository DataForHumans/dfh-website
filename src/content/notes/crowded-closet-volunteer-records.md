Crowded Closet is a nonprofit thrift shop in Iowa City, Iowa. Hundreds of volunteers sort donations, hang clothes, test electronics, and run the registers, and the shop's profits go to Mennonite Central Committee's relief work and to local community agencies. The shop has been part of Iowa City since 1978, and it runs on people who give their time.

That makes the volunteer program the center of the operation, and it makes the volunteer office's time one of the shop's scarcest resources. When we met Eva Anderson, the volunteer manager, her team faced a question many nonprofits know well: with a small staff and more opportunities than hours, where should recruiting effort go?

## The challenge

Crowded Closet's volunteer program has grown rapidly over the years, and its records have grown with it. The shop tracks volunteers in a scheduling system built to manage shifts, and over time it had collected nearly a thousand volunteer records. The technology had not kept pace with that growth. The scheduling system does not connect to the shop's email, its texting tool, or its Google Contacts, so one person's information often lived in several places and sometimes disagreed with itself. Old landline numbers sat in records for people who now use only a cell phone. Common last names produced mixed-up contacts. Many volunteers had no recorded church, school, or group affiliation, even though staff knew plenty of those connections from personal relationships.

The volunteer office had good instincts about who showed up. What it lacked was a way to check those instincts against the records, and a way to see the whole program at once.

## What we did

We started by learning how the data was collected and what each field meant. That took more than one conversation. A field labeled for organization and church meant something different in practice than it did on screen, and the shop's flags for volunteer type mixed roles such as cashier with affiliations such as a particular congregation. Sorting that out with the team came first.

From there we analyzed the records and built charts for the team to react to: who is serving, how many hours different groups contribute, and where affiliation information was missing. We reviewed the findings together in a working session and turned them into practical next steps. Those included lists of records to fix, ordered so staff could begin with volunteers who had served this year, a mapping of the shop's flags to a cleaner set of affiliation categories, and revised spreadsheets to help clean up Google Contacts. We also offered ideas the patterns suggested, such as reaching out to volunteers with substantial past hours who had gone quiet, and designing separate opportunities for different kinds of volunteers.

## What changed

The finding that mattered most was about age. The charts showed that volunteers 60 and older provide a large share of the shop's consistent, long-term service. Younger volunteers, especially students, contribute energy and speed over shorter stretches. The team had always valued both groups. The analysis gave them a way to weigh the two when time was tight.

It changed decisions within months. Invited to staff a table at a large university event, the volunteer office declined. The event would have taken a staff member out of the shop for half a day, and students tend to find the shop on their own. Instead, Eva spoke at a nearby senior living community. By the fall, the community had launched a monthly volunteer pilot with nine volunteer slots, and a group of residents arrived for their first two-hour shift. She also spoke to a local Lions Club, and a colleague has scheduled visits through the winter with church groups and women's sewing circles.

The team made smaller changes as well. They revised the volunteer application, replacing the old organization and church field with one labeled Faith Community. They gave student volunteers a shorter path in: a half-hour orientation, then straight onto a first shift. They reviewed volunteer statuses, moved people who no longer serve to inactive, and began archiving older records so the system reflects who is currently engaged. And they started personally reaching out to past volunteers who once gave many hours.

In the team's own words, the analysis helped them move "from recognizing the value of community outreach to making it a greater priority in our recruitment strategy."

## Still in progress

Data cleanup is never finished. Eva compared it to laundry: it is always waiting. The scheduling system and Google Contacts still do not sync, so keeping contact details current remains a steady job. The team is now looking at a yearly check in which volunteers confirm their own information, better ways to separate regular, occasional, and at-home volunteers, and simple tools for tracking relationships alongside schedules. They are also working out how to share volunteer numbers back with the volunteers themselves, who respond when they see what their hours add up to.

## What it shows

Nothing here required a large system. The shop already had the records. It needed someone to organize them, ask what the fields meant, and bring findings back in a form the team could act on. Then a staff member with deep roots in her community did the part no analysis can: she walked into a room and invited people to volunteer.
